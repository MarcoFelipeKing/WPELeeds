Mapping Unsafely Managed Sanitation Around the World

How a visual tool co-developed at Leeds helped rewrite the global sanitation target and shaped more than US$1.1 billion in urban infrastructure investment.

Sanitation & WASH Research Group

The Challenge

Lead Researchers: Professor Barbara Evans (Chair in Public Health Engineering), Professor Andy Sleigh, and DrPaul Hutchings
Key Partners: WHO, UNICEF, Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, World Bank, Eawag/Sandec, GIZ, CSE India, WaterAid
Global Reach: Adopted in 100+ cities across Asia, Africa, and Latin America; over US$1.1 billion in urban sanitation investment informed

Under the Millennium Development Goals, sanitation progress was measured largely by one question: does a household have a toilet? That measure hid a much larger problem. In rapidly growing cities across Africa, Asia, and Latin America, most households use on-site systems such as pit latrines or septic tanks, or connect to sewers that discharge without treatment. Having a toilet at home does not prevent disease if the waste is then dumped into a neighbourhood drain, canal, or river.

Municipal engineers and city mayors knew the problem existed, but lacked a straightforward way to quantify where excreta went after leaving the household — or to explain the scale of the failure to finance ministries and development banks.

The Research

Research led by Professor Barbara Evans (Chair in Public Health Engineering) and Professor Andy Sleigh at the University of Leeds examined the entire urban sanitation service chain — from containment at the household, through emptying and transport, to treatment and final disposal or reuse.

Working with international partners including the World Bank Water and Sanitation Program, GIZ, Eawag/Sandec, the Centre for Science and Environment (India), and WaterAid through the Shit Flow Diagram (SFD) Promotion Initiative, the Leeds team co-developed a standardised methodology and open-access graphic generator that traces excreta flows through a city. The resulting diagram shows, at a glance, the proportion of a city's faecal waste that is safely managed (green) versus the proportion that is discharged untreated into the urban environment (red), and pinpoints where along the service chain the system fails.

Alongside the SFD work, Professor Evans served as senior technical adviser to the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme (JMP) Sanitation Working Group, providing the engineering and public health evidence base for how global sanitation targets should be defined and monitored after 2015.

Global Impact

01

Redefining the Global Sanitation Target (SDG 6.2)

Leeds research directly informed the shift from measuring basic toilet access under the MDGs to measuring "safely managed sanitation services" under UN Sustainable Development Goal Target 6.2, adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2015 and monitored globally by WHO and UNICEF.

02

Transforming City Planning in Over 100 Cities

The SFD methodology and graphic generator — hosted on the Sustainable Sanitation Alliance (SuSanA) platform — became the standard diagnostic tool for urban sanitation planning worldwide, used by municipal authorities, utilities, NGOs, and consultants across more than 100 cities.

03

Shaping Major Infrastructure Investment

SFDs and the underlying service-chain framework were adopted by the Bill & Melinda Gates Foundation, the World Bank, the Asian Development Bank, and national governments (including India's National Policy on Faecal Sludge and Septage Management), informing more than US$1.1 billion in urban sanitation programmes that prioritise non-sewered faecal sludge management alongside conventional sewerage.

04

Who Benefits

Urban residents in low- and middle-income cities; municipal utilities and city planners; national ministries of urban development, water, and health; and international development agencies including WHO, UNICEF, the World Bank, GIZ, and WaterAid.
